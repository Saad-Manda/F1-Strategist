Page 03 / 11 — A little math

The Checkpoint Pool as Approximate Fictitious Self-Play

Storing weights doesn't let you literally average policies the way you can average a mixed strategy in a matrix game — so the real plan approximates the average with a sampled pool instead.

Why "average the strategies" needs a stand-in for a DQN

Page 02's convergence argument depended on tracking a running average of the opponent's strategy. That's a clean operation for a matrix game, where a strategy is just a probability over a handful of actions. It doesn't translate directly to a DQN: there's no meaningful way to "average" the weights of ten different neural networks and expect the result to behave like the average of their policies. The practical fix: instead of averaging weights, keep a pool of past policy snapshots (checkpoints) and sample among them — an empirical distribution over past strategies that approximates fictitious play's running average without ever averaging a single weight.

The real plan's sampling rule

Not a uniform sample over the whole pool, either. The real plan's CheckpointPool.sample() uses a recency-biased rule: with probability recency_bias = 0.7, sample uniformly from the most recent 20% of the pool; otherwise (probability 0.3), sample uniformly from the older 80%.

pool size n = 50 recent slice = most recent 20% of 50 = 10 checkpoints (n − int(0.8·n) = 50 − 40) older slice = remaining 80% of 50 = 40 checkpoints P(sample any one specific recent checkpoint) = 0.7 / 10 = 0.07 (7% each) P(sample any one specific older checkpoint) = 0.3 / 40 = 0.0075 (0.75% each) Per-checkpoint weighting, recent : older = 0.07 / 0.0075 ≈ 9.3 : 1

Roughly a 9:1 per-checkpoint weighting of recent versus old — not a flat average across all 50. This is a deliberate departure from page 02's pure running-average argument, and worth naming as a trade-off rather than a derived-from-first-principles number:

  • Too much weight on ancient checkpoints wastes training signal on opponents the learner has long since outgrown — every episode spent beating a policy from 15,000 episodes ago teaches little about the current frontier.
  • Too little weight on old checkpoints risks "chasing the tail" — re-learning only to beat the newest policy while forgetting how to beat older styles, exactly the cycling failure page 02's worked example showed for best-responding to the single latest strategy alone.

Recency bias is the practical middle ground: strongly favor the frontier, but never fully forget the pool's history.

The self-play evaluation trap

A self-play agent's training reward curve going up only proves it's getting better at beating its own recent history — it says nothing about whether it would beat a genuinely different strategy it's never encountered, such as a Phase 3 rule-based agent (assumed background) or a human's intuition. This is exactly why the real plan's Definition of Done checks the trained learner against something outside the self-play loop entirely ("learner's mean finishing position < random agent's"), rather than trusting the self-play reward curve on its own. A rising curve inside a closed loop is evidence of progress relative to that loop, not evidence of skill in any absolute sense.
