Overtaking and Dirty Air

Two small formulas stand in for an aerodynamic effect and a racing decision. Both are about the car in front.

Your call · the first five laps

On the Pit Wall with blocking on, you start 0.3 s behind a rival that is 0.3 s a lap slower. Within 1 s you can try to pass on any lap you are faster, and in its wake you lose time.

How often do you pass it in the first five laps?

Dirty air

A car following closely loses downforce in the wake of the car ahead, more the closer it is. The plan models it as a straight line: DIRTY_AIR_MAX_PENALTY = 0.5 s at zero gap, falling to 0 at DIRTY_AIR_MAX_GAP = 1.5 s and beyond.

penalty(gap) = 0 if gap ≥ 1.5 penalty(gap) = 0.5 · (1.5 − gap) / 1.5 if gap < 1.5 gap 0.00 → 0.500 gap 0.75 → 0.250 gap 1.50 → 0.000

The three values are the ones the plan's own tests assert, checkable by hand.

Probabilistic overtaking

A pass can only be tried within OVERTAKE_ATTEMPT_GAP = 1.0 s, and only by a car faster this lap: pace_delta < 0, with pace_delta = my_lap_time − their_lap_time. Given an attempt, it succeeds with a sigmoid in the pace advantage:

base_prob(pace_delta) = 1 / (1 + exp(5 · (pace_delta + 0.5))) pace_delta = −0.5: exp(0) = 1 → 1 / 2 = 50%

On top of that, follow_bonus = min(0.1 × laps_following, 0.2) rewards persistence, and the total is capped at 0.95, so no pass is ever certain. The Pit Wall uses the sigmoid and the cap, without the follow bonus.

The numbers sayNever

At 0.3 s behind, the wake costs 0.5 × 1.2 / 1.5 = 0.40 s, more than your 0.3 s edge. You are slower than the rival on that lap, so pace_delta is positive and no attempt is made. So the gap grows a little each lap, 0.40, 0.47, 0.51, 0.54 and 0.56 s after the first five, and the wake shrinks with it. It settles at 0.6 s, where the wake costs 0.3 s and the two cars run at the same pace.

The faster car is stuck, by the wake alone. With blocking on the Pit Wall puts the wake in the lap time, so being behind costs time. In the plan's race the wake reaches the score but not the lap time (page 06, E3), so it can't hold anyone back, and passing has nothing to release you from.

Check the docstring against the formula

The plan's docstring also claims 80% at 1.0 s faster and 95% at 1.5 s faster. The formula gives 1 / (1 + e^−2.5) ≈ 92.4% and 1 / (1 + e^−5) ≈ 99.3%, which the cap then holds to 95%. A steeper curve than advertised is still a fair model. The habit matters more than the numbers: hand-check a formula against its own comment before trusting either, and expect pass rates in training logs to follow the formula.

A modeling choice, not a law of physics

The sigmoid and its constants are one reasonable form among many; a step or another curve would do. What matters is what it captures: a bigger pace edge means a likelier pass, and following for a while helps a little. Know that well enough to defend or retune the constants later.

Evidence so farThe wake and the pass both depend on the car ahead, and either one could make position matter. With the wake in the lap time, a faster car settles 0.6 s behind and never gets an attempt. In the plan's race the wake never reaches the lap time. 4 · Reading the model

A car 0.3 s a lap faster on its own sits 0.3 s behind, inside the wake. Its lap time is taken to include the wake loss. How likely is a pass this lap?
You want passing to be harder without changing which cars may attempt it. Which constant do you change?
Worked solution

Wake: 0.4 s at 0.3 s, 0.1 s at 1.2 s. Pass at 0.8 s faster: 1 / (1 + e^−1.5) = 81.8%; with three laps following, 81.8% + 20% capped to 95%. The docstring's 80% at 1.0 s needs k = 2 ln 4 = 2.77, which gives 94.1% at 1.5 s. A 0.3 s faster car in a 0.4 s wake is not faster this lap, so it gets no attempt. Steepness changes how often, the attempt gap changes who.
