Page 04 / 12 — A little math

PPO's Clipped Surrogate Objective

The last of idea.md's four listed concepts, and the trick that makes reusing one batch of experience for multiple gradient steps safe.

The problem: a small step in parameters can be a huge step in behavior

Pages 01–03 built a gradient estimator, but nothing about it prevents a single update from moving θ far enough to change the policy's behavior catastrophically. If the policy is already fairly close to deterministic — say, "almost always pit on lap 18" — a small change in the underlying logits can flip it to "almost never pit on lap 18," with no way back, since the next batch of experience is now collected under a badly broken policy. This is qualitatively different from a bad update in Q-learning (Phase 4/5, assumed background): a wrong TD update to one state's Q-value doesn't usually cause this kind of behavioral collapse, because acting greedily against a slightly-wrong value function is comparatively forgiving.

The probability ratio

PPO measures how far a candidate update has moved the policy, for a given (s,a), using the ratio between the new and old action probabilities:

r_t(θ) = π_θ(a_t|s_t) / π_θ_old(a_t|s_t)

π_θ_old is the fixed policy that actually collected the current batch of experience — the ratio is only ever computed relative to that snapshot. This is exactly why PPO is on-policy: r_t(θ) is only meaningful for data collected under a policy close to the current one, unlike Phase 5's replay buffer, which reuses arbitrarily old off-policy transitions without any such correction.

The surrogate objective, and why it's dangerous unclipped

The natural objective to maximize is r_t(θ) · A_t (using page 03's GAE estimate for A_t) — increase the probability of actions with positive advantage, decrease it for negative advantage, weighted by how much the ratio has already moved. Left unclipped, this is exactly as dangerous as the naive gradient from page 01: a large ratio times a large positive advantage produces an enormous, destabilizing push in exactly the direction that risks the collapse described above.

The clip

L^CLIP(θ) = min( r_t(θ)·A_t , clip(r_t(θ), 1−ε, 1+ε)·A_t )

with the real plan's ε = 0.2 (clip_range in configs/ppo.yaml), so the clip range is [0.8, 1.2]. The clip is deliberately asymmetric in effect, depending on the sign of A_t — worked through both signs below at ε = 0.2:

The pattern the two tables reveal: for a positive advantage, the clip only ever binds when r_t has grown past 1+ε — it caps how much the objective can reward pushing an already-good action's probability even higher, but never penalizes pushing it lower than the old policy had it. For a negative advantage, the clip only ever binds when r_t has shrunk past 1−ε — it caps how much the objective can reward suppressing an already-bad action's probability further, but never penalizes raising it. In both cases the effect is the same: once a change has moved far enough in the direction the advantage already favors, the objective stops offering any further reward for moving it more — precisely the behavior that prevents an enormous, destabilizing update.

Why this makes reusing a batch across multiple epochs safe

The real plan's n_epochs: 10 means each collected batch of rollout data is used for 10 separate passes of gradient updates before new data is collected. For a vanilla, unclipped policy gradient this would be unsound — after a few epochs of updates on the same batch, the policy could drift far enough from the one that actually collected the data that the whole estimator becomes meaningless. The clip is exactly what makes this safe: however many epochs are spent on one batch, no single step can push r_t(θ) usefully far outside [1−ε, 1+ε], so the policy is structurally prevented from drifting too far from π_θ_old no matter how many epochs are spent trying.
