Expectations Over Randomness

Page 2's toy race had no luck in it — every action led to exactly one next state. A real race doesn't get that guarantee: a safety car might arrive, or it might not. This page is the one new piece of math that closes that gap.

What changes when the next state isn't certain

Page 2's recursion assumed each state-action pair leads to exactly one successor state. Once a safety car can arrive at random, that's no longer true: from a given green-flag state, taking STAY leads to two possible next states — still green, or now under a safety car — and the DP has no way to know in advance which one it'll be. The general backward-induction equation already accounted for this with an expectation, written but not yet unpacked on page 2:

V_t(s) = min_a [ cost(s, a) + E_s'[ V_(t+1)(s') ] ]

Unpacking E_s'[...] is this page's whole job. Because there are only two possible outcomes here — safety car arrives, or it doesn't — the expectation is nothing more exotic than a weighted average of two known numbers:

E_s'[ V_(t+1)(s') ] = P(sc_next = 1) · V_(t+1)(s' with sc_active=1) + P(sc_next = 0) · V_(t+1)(s' with sc_active=0)

This is the defining move of stochastic dynamic programming: wherever the deterministic recursion referenced "the" next value, the stochastic version references a probability-weighted average over every next value that could actually occur. Everything else about the backward pass — working from V_(T+1)=0 toward lap 1, one lap at a time — is completely unchanged.

Where the probabilities come from: a hazard-rate model

The two probabilities above need their own small model, calibrated from real safety-car data in Phase 0.5. Treat each green lap as a coin flip with a small, per-circuit chance of triggering a safety car — a standard way to model "how likely is a rare event on any given opportunity" (the same idea survival analysis calls a hazard rate):

P(sc starts next lap | currently green) = hazard (a small per-lap probability, fit per circuit) P(sc continues next lap | currently active) = 1 − 1 / mean_duration (mean_duration in laps)

The second line is a standard property of this kind of model: if a safety car period is expected to last mean_duration laps on average, treating each lap under it as an independent chance to end gives exactly a 1/mean_duration chance of ending on any given lap, so 1 − 1/mean_duration is the chance it's still running next lap.

Real safety-car frequency varies enormously by circuit — historically, street circuits like Singapore have seen a safety car in nearly every race, while Bahrain sees one in only a small fraction. That's exactly why hazard is fit per circuit in Phase 0.5, rather than assumed to be one universal constant: a single hazard rate applied everywhere would make the oracle's optimal pit windows equally wrong at both extremes.

Worked example: how much does the risk of a safety car change today's decision?

Take page 2's lap-3, tyre-age-2 cell and ask what changes if there's now a 25% chance a safety car arrives on lap 4. Suppose (from a version of the recursion that also tracks sc_active) the two possible V_4 values are already known: V_4(age=3, green) = 16.0 — page 2's own number — and a new one, V_4(sc_active) = 20.0, reflecting that a safety-car lap is slow and pitting under it, discounted, still isn't worth it with no laps left afterward:

E[V_4 | currently green, hazard = 0.25] = 0.25 · V_4(sc_active) + 0.75 · V_4(age=3, green) = 0.25 · 20.0 + 0.75 · 16.0 = 5.0 + 12.0 = 17.0 V_3(age=2, STAY) = lap_time(age=2) + E[V_4] = 13.0 + 17.0 = 30.0

Compare that to page 2's fully deterministic answer for the same cell: 13 + V_4(3) = 13 + 16 = 29. The one-lap risk of a slow safety-car lap next lap raises today's expected cost by exactly 1.0 — a small, honest markup, computed the same way a race strategist reasons informally: "there's a real chance of a safety car soon, and that changes what staying out is actually worth, even though it hasn't happened yet." Nothing about how to compute a backward pass changed from page 2 — only that every reference to "the next value" became a weighted average over the outcomes that could occur.

The pit-under-safety-car discount, and why it belongs in the transition, not the reward

The Phase 3.5 plan specifies pitting under a safety car costs roughly 40% of the normal pit loss, because the whole field is circulating slowly and the relative time lost by ducking into the pits is much smaller. That's a fact about cost(s, a) — the immediate term in the recursion, computed by dynamics.compute_pit_loss — not about the expectation over next states covered above. Getting the two mixed up is an easy mistake with a large consequence, which is exactly why the design doc calls it out explicitly: zeroing the reward on safety-car laps (rather than discounting the immediate pit-loss term) would also zero out the pit-loss cost, making an SC pit look free instead of merely cheap, and collapsing the optimal policy into "always wait for a safety car" — a strategy that only looks good because of an accounting error, not because it's actually cheap.

Why compute_lap_time(noise=0) is exact, not approximate

Backward induction needs a single number for cost(s,a) at every state — not a distribution. But dynamics.compute_lap_time, run inside the live environment, adds a small random noise term to every lap for realism. The DP oracle is allowed to sidestep that noise entirely, and the justification is a two-line argument, not a shortcut taken on faith:

lap_time = deterministic_part(state, action) + noise, where E[noise] = 0 E[lap_time] = E[deterministic_part] + E[noise] = deterministic_part + 0 = deterministic_part = compute_lap_time(..., noise=0)

Because the noise term is additive and zero-mean by construction, dropping it doesn't approximate the expected lap time — it is the expected lap time, exactly. This is precisely why the Phase 3.5 plan insists the DP oracle call dynamics.compute_lap_time itself with noise disabled, rather than reimplementing the lap-time formula: any independent reimplementation would need to separately prove this same zero-mean property for whatever noise model it assumed, and a drift between two "equivalent" formulas is exactly the kind of bug that looks individually correct in each file and is nearly invisible in review.

This is also why the oracle's rollout evaluation (page 6) has to be checked against its own V[s_0]: V[s_0] is the expected race time under the model — computed exactly, using this zero-noise shortcut, throughout the whole backward pass. The rollout mean, run with noise switched back on in the real environment, is the measured value under the actual simulator. If those two numbers diverge by more than sampling noise would explain, either the expectation math above has a bug, or the environment and the oracle have quietly drifted apart — and the test exists specifically to catch whichever one it is.
