Phase 8 · Page 07 · Is it bimodal?

Is It Really Bimodal?

A histogram that looks two-humped and a histogram that is evidence of cars reacting to each other are not the same thing.

Your call · a shared safety car

2,000 synthetic races. Each car stops in its own window, and in half the races a safety car comes out and a car whose window is near it stops under it. Neither car ever reacts to the other. You test for a reaction by shuffling which race each rival stop is paired with.

What does the test say?

The plan's analysis

For every stop the trained agent makes, record its lap minus the nearest rival stop's lap, over many evaluation races, and plot the histogram. Look for peaks at small negative deltas (undercuts) and small positive ones (overcuts), with a dip at 0.

"Looks bimodal" is weaker than it sounds. Both cars' stops cluster around preferred stint lengths anyway: the tyre model gives every car a window, whatever any other car does. Two cars that each like laps 18 to 22 put many deltas near zero with no reaction at all.

A null: shuffle the pairing

Keep the agent's stop laps and the rival's, but pair each agent stop with a rival stop from a random other race, and recompute the histogram. Real deltas come from cars that raced each other and could react; shuffled ones come from cars that never shared a race. A reaction should show as a deeper dip and taller near-zero peaks than the shuffle can produce. This is a permutation test: compare a statistic with the same statistic on data randomised to destroy only the link being tested.

real_deltas = [agent_lap[i] − rival_lap[i] for each race i] shuffled_rival = permutation(rival_lap) null_deltas = [agent_lap[i] − shuffled_rival[i] for each race i]

The reveal · a shared safety car

You calledno call made
The numbers sayA reaction, at p = 0.002

Both cars stop on the same lap in 0.261 of races; the shuffle gives 0.093, and the test rejects. The cars look linked because they answered the same safety car. The shuffle broke that shared cause along with any reaction.

The fix: shuffle only among races with the same safety-car lap (or none). Within such a group the shared cause is the same for every race, so a shuffle breaks only the link between the cars. On the same data: null 0.258, p = 0.67, no rejection. With a real undercut-or-overcut reaction added, the stratified test still rejects at p = 0.002. In the real plan every car in a race shares its scenario, so this is the normal case: shuffle among races with the same scenario seed.

What the test can and can't show

The shuffle answers one question: did one car's stop depend on the other's in the same race? It doesn't answer "is the histogram's shape strategic?". At a mixed equilibrium of plans fixed before the start, such as the duel's, each car's stop lap is an independent draw from its mixture. The delta histogram is strategic through and through, undercuts on one side, an overcut on the other, and a dip at 0, yet the shuffle reproduces it and the test doesn't reject (p = 0.93). The shape lives in each car's own distribution, which the shuffle keeps.

So the analysis needs both comparisons: the stratified shuffle for "did they react?", and the same training with blocking off for "does the shape need blocking?" (page 06). And both are read over several training seeds. On the duel, learners from four seeds of one configuration put anywhere from 9% to 78% of their stops on the undercut side, against an exact 61%.

Evidence so farEven with blocking, one run's histogram proves little: a plain shuffle calls a shared safety car a reaction, a strategic shape can pass the shuffle, and four seeds of one setup spread from 9% to 78%. The histogram needs a stratified null, a blocking-off run and several seeds. Next: you solve the duel with no noise.
