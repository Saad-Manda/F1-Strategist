What "Doing Well" Means Now

Phase 1's reward was the cleanest this project will have. Racing for position costs that.

Your call · a reward for finishing ahead

The plan's first reward design adds 20 s to the score for each place gained at the flag. Against the lap-9 rival in the plan's race, that is 20 s for finishing ahead of it.

Does the bonus move the best stop away from lap 8?

The reward Phases 1 to 5 had

−lap_time, every lap. Summed over a race it equals minus the race time: the per-lap reward is the objective, not a stand-in for it. The only open questions were about learning it well.

The honest reward for racing well is sparse

If the goal becomes finishing as high as possible, the honest reward is the final position. It exists, but it is sparse: known once, at the flag, after every call of the race has been made. Did lap 14's stop help the P3 finish, or lap 31's tyre choice, or lap 40's pass? That is the credit-assignment problem. TD learning (Phase 4) passes value back one step at a time, but a reward of zero for fifty laps and one number at the end gives each update very little to work with, compared with a lap time on every lap.

Two dense stand-ins

  • lap_time_plus_position: −lap_time every lap, plus position_bonus_scale = 20 seconds-equivalent per place gained, on the last step. Mostly the honest signal, with a nudge toward position at the end.
  • gap_change: every lap, −(curr_gap − prev_gap), the gap to the leader starting from 0. Dense, but a stand-in.

The plan's worry about gap_change is that an agent paid for closing gaps every lap will burn its tyres for a short gain. Check that against the arithmetic. With γ = 1 each lap's change cancels the next one's start, and the sum is minus the final gap. (It stays true through a safety car: the plan skips the reward on those laps but still updates prev_gap.) A gap closed now and lost later earns nothing overall. gap_change pays for the final margin to the leader, in instalments: a different objective from race time, not a short-sighted version of it. The general risk stands, and it has a name, reward hacking: once a stand-in is scored, raising its number and doing what it was meant to measure can come apart. With γ < 1, early instalments also count for more than late ones, and the sum stops matching the margin.

The numbers sayNo: lap 8 stays best

In the plan's race the rival finishes in 231.37 s whatever you do: nothing you do touches its lap times. Every one-stop from lap 4 to lap 11 beats that, so every plan near the top already gets the bonus, and it adds the same 20 s to each. The ranking under them is the ranking by race time, with lap 8 first.

gap_change agrees: its sum is 231.37 s minus your time, so it too ranks plans by your time alone. Every design in the plan picks lap 8 in the plan's race. A reward can only make the rival matter through something the rival affects, and here it affects nothing of yours.

Why the plan trains three designs

The plan doesn't argue its way to one design. It trains three, lap_time_plus_position, gap_change and potential_shaping (page 03), and compares what each produces: different pit timing (the average tyre age at a stop), faster convergence, a better race. Trying the tradeoff and reporting the result is good practice, and docs/idea.md warns why: a rising learning curve proves nothing by itself, because the agent may be farming a flaw in the reward.

Evidence so farEvery reward design in the plan picks lap 8 in the plan's race. The rival's 231.37 s is fixed, so finishing ahead is a line your own time crosses or doesn't. Not the reward, then. Next: a shaping term that pays for position every lap.
