From an Equation to an Update Rule

The oracle priced lap 8 from the rules. A learner has to price it from laps it has driven.

Your call · lap 8, in training

A learner stays out on lap 8. The lap takes 11.36 s, and lap 9 starts under green. It has never seen the lap-time formula or the 6% safety-car chance.

What does it do with its estimate for staying out on lap 8?

The equation hasn't changed

Phase 1 wrote the Bellman equation, and Phase 3.5 solved it by working back from the flag:

Q*(s, a) = Σ_s′ P(s′ | s, a) · [ R(s, a, s′) + γ · max_a′ Q*(s′, a′) ]

The oracle could do the sum because P and R were written down. A learner can't. It acts, and the race hands back one next state, the one that happened. There is no sum over every possible s′, only the one sample.

Nudge, don't solve

The temporal-difference (TD) idea (Sutton & Barto, chapter 6) uses that one real s′ in place of the sum, and moves the estimate a small step toward it:

target = r + γ · (estimate for s′) δ = target − Q(s, a) the TD error: how wrong was the old guess? Q(s, a) ← Q(s, a) + α · δ α, the learning rate, sets the step

One lap is a noisy glimpse of what usually happens. Moving all the way to it, which is option (c), makes the table chase noise. A small step, repeated over many races, lets the noise average out. Every rule in this phase has this shape. Q-learning (page 02) and SARSA (page 04) differ in one place only: what goes into target.

The reveal · lap 8

The numbers sayA small step toward the sample

Lap 8's target is 11.36 + 141.79 = 153.15 s when lap 9 is green, and 11.36 + 145.08 = 156.44 s when it starts behind the safety car. Feed those to the update with α = 0.1, in the race's own proportions, 94 in 100 and 6 in 100, and the estimate settles at 153.34 s, the oracle's number, with a spread of 0.18 s. The learner never saw the 6%. The small steps averaged it in.

Option (c) jumps to whichever lap it saw last: 153.15 or 156.44, never the average. Option (a) is a real method, Monte Carlo, which waits for the flag. The next section says why TD doesn't.

Learning from a guess about a guess

Look at what the target is made of: a real lap time r, plus the table's estimate for the next state, which is still being learned. Updating one estimate from another is called bootstrapping. It is what separates TD methods from Monte Carlo, which waits for the flag and uses the real total.

Bootstrapping lets the table learn on every lap instead of once a race: a real race is 52 laps, so waiting for the flag would waste 51 updates. The cost is that an update can be built on a value that is still wrong. The table gets there by repeated correction over many races, not because any single update is right. Hold on to that: page 06 shows what happens when the value it bootstraps from can never be right.

Deep diveHow the step size trades speed for noise80 words

The same lap-8 experiment, 1,000 laps, repeated 2,000 times. With α = 0.5 the estimate averages 153.34 s but wanders with a spread of 0.45 s: each lap moves it halfway. With α = 0.1 the spread is 0.18 s; with α = 0.01, 0.05 s. The price of a small α is speed: starting from 0, α = 0.1 has reached 99.75 after 10 laps and 152.71 after 50. A little at a time

The page says to move an estimate "a little at a time". Here you choose how little, on one table cell, and see what each choice costs. Then you sort four updates into bootstrapped and not.

1 · One cell, one noisy value

A single cell starts at 0 and is shown 200 noisy samples of a quantity whose true value is −10 (each sample is off by up to 4 either way). After each sample it applies estimate ← estimate + α · (sample − estimate). The dots are the samples and the line is the estimate. Drag α.

Which of these has the smallest spread over the last 100 samples?

2 · The same thing, as arithmetic

Leave the noise out. If every sample were exactly the target, each update would close a fraction α of whatever gap is left, so after n updates the fraction of the original gap closed is 1 − (1 − α)ⁿ.

3 · Bootstrapped or not?

An update is bootstrapped if its target contains an estimate. It is Monte Carlo if its target is made only of rewards that were actually observed.

Worked solution

1. α = 1.00: spread 7.93. α = 0.05: within 1 of the true value from sample 50, spread 1.20. α = 0.01: never arrives in 200 samples (it ends at −8.68), and its spread of 2.42 is drift, not noise. The smallest spread of the three is at 0.05.

2. 1 − 0.9¹⁰ = 65.1%. 1 − 0.99²⁰⁰ = 86.6%.

3. Bootstrapped, Monte Carlo, Monte Carlo (the terminal target has no estimate in it), bootstrapped.
