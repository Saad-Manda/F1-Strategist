The Game Underneath: Nash Equilibria and Fictitious Self-Play

Self-play isn't really chasing "the optimal policy" — it's chasing a solution to a two-player game, which is a different, relative notion of "solved."

Why "optimal policy" stops being the right target

Every phase through Phase 6 asked for the policy that maximizes expected return against a fixed environment — a well-defined, single best answer. Self-play changes what's being maximized against: each agent picks a strategy — really, a full pit-timing policy, not a single action — and the return either agent gets depends on both agents' strategies at once. That's a two-player game, not a single-agent MDP, and "optimal" no longer has a fixed target to be optimal relative to.

Nash equilibrium

The natural notion of "solved" for a game like this: a pair of strategies where neither player can improve their own return by unilaterally switching strategy, holding the other player's strategy fixed.

(π_A*, π_B*) is a Nash equilibrium if Return_A(π_A*, π_B*) ≥ Return_A(π_A, π_B*) for every alternative π_A Return_B(π_A*, π_B*) ≥ Return_B(π_A*, π_B) for every alternative π_B

Neither side has a unilateral incentive to deviate. This is a genuinely different concept from Phase 4's optimal policy: there, "optimal" meant best against a fixed world; here, each player's "best" depends on what the other player is doing, so the two definitions have to hold simultaneously, each in terms of the other.

Fictitious self-play

A classical way to actually find such a pair: each player repeatedly computes a best response — the strategy that maximizes their own return — not against the other player's single latest strategy, but against the average of the other player's strategies over all rounds played so far. Under broad conditions (provably, in zero-sum two-player games), this process converges to a Nash equilibrium. This is the theoretical backbone the checkpoint pool (page 03) is a practical approximation of.

Why "best-respond to the latest only" doesn't converge: a worked example

A small 2×2 reduction of rock-paper-scissors — two strategies each, Rock (R) and Paper (P), payoffs to the row player:

col=R col=P row=R: 0 −1 row=P: +1 0

Rock loses to Paper, Paper beats Rock, same-vs-same ties at 0. Start both players on Rock. Best-respond to the opponent's single latest strategy, alternating turns:

Round 1: both play R (tie, 0 each) Round 2: row best-responds to col's R → row switches to P (P beats R) col best-responds to row's R → col switches to P (same instant, using round 1's info) Round 3: row best-responds to col's P → row has to avoid P... but row's only options are R, P; against P, R loses and P ties. Row switches to R would lose, so row is stuck oscillating with col, each chasing what the other just played last round, never settling.

Best-responding to the single latest strategy cycles — the classic "rock beats scissors beats paper beats rock" chase, here just R and P chasing each other forever, never settling on anything.

Now best-respond to the running average of the opponent's play instead. Track each player's empirical mix of R vs. P over all rounds so far, and best-respond to that average mixture rather than the single last move:

Round 1: both play R. Averages after rd 1: row = 100% R, col = 100% R Round 2: best-respond to opponent's average (100% R) → both switch fully to P. Averages after rd 2: row = 50% R / 50% P, col = 50% R / 50% P Round 3: best-respond to a 50/50 average → indifferent between R and P (both score 0 in expectation against a 50/50 mix) → each player can now mix, e.g. play R and P alternately. Averages keep drifting toward 50/50 R/P for both players as rounds continue.

The running average's proportions are visibly closing in on a 50/50 mixture for both players — the actual (mixed-strategy) Nash equilibrium of this reduced game, where neither player can improve by deviating once both are playing 50/50. Best-responding to history, not to the latest snapshot, is what makes this converge instead of cycle.

Population-based training, in one paragraph

AlphaStar (DeepMind), referenced by name in the real plan's own docstring, is the large-scale descendant of this same idea: instead of tracking one running average opponent, maintain a whole population of diverse policies and train against a mixture of them, which both approximates fictitious play's averaging and adds robustness against being over-fit to any single opponent style. This is the direct motivation for why the real plan keeps up to 50 checkpoints in a pool, rather than training against only "the current best" — the next page works through exactly how that pool approximates the running-average idea above with discrete, sampled snapshots instead of literal averaging.
