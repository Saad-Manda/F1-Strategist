A rare event can dominate a mean

Compare SAFE, costing 4 every time, with RISKY, costing 1 with probability 0.99 and 400 with probability 0.01. RISKY's expected cost is 0.99×1 + 0.01×400 = 4.99. A risk-neutral cost minimizer chooses SAFE. A short run of RISKY may look excellent because most samples cost 1.

In 100 independent RISKY draws, the chance of seeing no expensive event is 0.99^100 ≈ 0.366. Zero observed failures is therefore weak evidence that the modeled one-percent event does not exist. Use the whole specified probability table for planning; use counts and uncertainty when estimating a table from data.

A mean does not enforce a risk limit

Change the expensive outcome to cost 100. RISKY's mean becomes 1.99, below SAFE's 4. The expectation objective now chooses RISKY, even though its worst case remains far worse. A requirement such as “failure probability below 0.005” would exclude it. Expected-cost optimization cannot silently stand in for that requirement.

Other objectives include worst-case cost, a tail average, or a probability constraint. They need their own definitions and often a different state or recurrence. Simply changing min to max over stochastic outcomes solves a different adversarial problem; it does not implement ordinary risk-neutral DP.

Check sensitivity before calling a benchmark exact

Suppose the fitted rare-event probability is uncertain. With cost 400 on failure and 1 otherwise, expected cost is 1+399p. SAFE's cost 4 is preferable when p > 3/399 ≈ 0.00752. A probability error of only a few tenths of a percentage point can reverse the decision near that threshold.

An exact solver uses its input probabilities correctly; it does not make those probabilities exact measurements of nature. Report fit counts, assumptions, sensitivity and evaluation data separately from arithmetic correctness. Avoid adjusting the model after seeing the final evaluation results and then presenting the same results as independent validation.

Recap and self-check

If ten samples all cost 1, should a planner delete the costly row?

No. It must distinguish an assumed model from estimated evidence. A short sample can easily miss a rare event; deleting it without a justified refit changes the task.
