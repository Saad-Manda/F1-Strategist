Experience Replay

A target network stops the target running away. It does nothing about a second problem: training on laps in the order they happened.

Your call · what the memory holds

The real agent keeps its last 100,000 laps and overwrites the oldest once full. A race is 52 laps.

After race 3,000, which is the oldest race the memory still holds?

Why in-order data is a problem

Consecutive laps of one race are much alike: similar tyre age, similar fuel, the same weather. Gradient descent behaves well when its examples are drawn independently from a steady distribution, i.i.d., and a stream of one race's laps in order is the opposite. If the last 20 updates all came from laps 30 to 50 of one race, the network has been told "late race, old tyres" 20 times running, and its weights drift to fit that slice at the expense of what they knew about the early laps. Page 03's problem was the target moving under the network. This one is the network moving because of what it is shown.

The fix: store, then sample

Store every transition (s, a, r, s′, done) in a memory as it happens, and train on a mini-batch drawn at random from the whole memory. That buys two separate things:

  • Less correlation. A random draw from thousands of transitions across many races is much closer to i.i.d. than one race in order.
  • Sample efficiency. Each stored transition can be trained on many times before it is overwritten, rather than once and discarded.

The baseline memory is a ring buffer: a fixed size, the oldest transition overwritten by the newest once full, and every stored transition equally likely to be drawn. Page 07 makes some transitions likelier than others.

The numbers sayAbout race 1,077

100,000 laps at 52 a race is about 1,923 races. After race 3,000, the 156,000th lap, the memory holds laps 56,001 to 156,000. Lap 56,001 is near the end of race 1,077, so the memory holds the last few laps of race 1,077 and all of races 1,078 to 3,000.

From race 1,923 on, each new lap pushes out the oldest. The network trains on its last two thousand races, not on everything it has seen.

What the champion's memory was fed

The capstone's schedule lowers ε from 1 to 0.05 over the first 60% of training, then holds it. The champion's last 800 races ran at ε = 0.05, a random call on about one lap in twenty, and every one of those laps went into its memory. That is the point of exploring in training: a call the greedy policy never makes still gets tried, stored and replayed, so its value is learned. Without those random stops the network couldn't know that lap 9's stop is worse.

Deep diveWhy a batch from the stream fails, in numbers100 words

On page 09's small world the bare learner trains on the one transition that just happened. In the runs made for that page its loss averaged 0.004 to 0.008 over the final 2,000 updates, and its spread was 0.014 to 0.046, several times its own average: a learner pulled about by whichever lap it saw last. With a memory and a target network the spread fell to 0.002 to 0.003. The races it found were the same on a world that small. On the capstone's 20-lap race with a mandatory second compound, the stream learner finishes far from the optimum.

A ring buffer, and what it does to a batch

You will build page 04's minimal replay buffer: fixed capacity, the oldest overwritten first, uniform sampling. Then you measure what it is for. Thirty-two laps taken in the order they were driven are strongly correlated, each one nearly a copy of the last. The same number of laps sampled from the buffer are not.
