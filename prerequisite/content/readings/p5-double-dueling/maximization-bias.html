Selecting the largest estimate changes its average

Two actions each have true value zero. Suppose each estimate independently takes −1 or +1 with equal probability. The four equally likely estimate pairs are [−1,−1], [−1,+1], [+1,−1], [+1,+1]. Their maxima are −1,+1,+1,+1, averaging 0.5. Each action's estimate individually averages zero, but the selected maximum averages above the largest true value, which is zero.

In symbols, averaging maxima is generally at least as large as maximizing averages: E[max_a estimate_a] ≥ max_a E[estimate_a]. The expectation averages over estimation noise. This is not a statement that every maximum is too high: the pair [−1,−1] is too low. Bias is an average property across the assumed estimation process.

Separate the chooser from its evaluator

If one noisy estimator chooses an action and an independent unbiased estimator supplies that action's value, the second does not inherit the first estimator's selected positive noise in this toy. In Double DQN, the online network chooses while the slower target network evaluates. Those networks are not truly independent; they share training history and copied parameters. The toy explains a source of optimism, not a theorem that the practical algorithm eliminates all bias.

Double DQN may reduce this effect, but can still have estimation error or underestimation. Different targets need not improve every task or every seed. Compare outcomes with matched data budgets, masks, reward units and evaluation policies. A network's numeric maximum alone cannot diagnose bias without a valid reference or repeated controlled estimates.

Recap and self-check

Do equal unbiased estimates imply their maximum is unbiased?

No. The maximum preferentially selects positive errors, as the four-pair calculation demonstrates. Selection and estimation are coupled.
