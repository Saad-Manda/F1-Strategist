Start where the future is known

In a four-lap toy race, lap cost is 10 + 3(age−1), with fresh age 1. STAY increases age by one. PIT adds 6 to the current old-tyre lap and makes the next age 1. There is no mandatory service, weather or randomness. We minimize total cost, so gamma is one.

Write C_t(age) for optimal remaining cost starting lap t. After lap four, C_5 = 0. On lap four PIT can never recover its extra cost; STAY wins at every reachable age. Hence C_4 at ages 1, 2, 3, 4 is [10, 13, 16, 19].

One earlier lap buys one future benefit

At lap three and age three, STAY costs 16 + C_4(4) = 35. PIT costs 16 + 6 + C_4(1) = 32. The pit costs six now but saves nine next lap. At age two both actions cost 29; at age one STAY costs 23 versus PIT 26.

The backward order matters: compute lap four first, then three, two and one. Each comparison uses already solved successors. A forward greedy decision compares only today's STAY and PIT cost, always avoids the pit, and pays 10+13+16+19 = 58.

Read the policy forward

Begin at lap one, age one. The stored policy says STAY; lap two, age two says PIT; lap three, age one says STAY; lap four, age two says STAY. Its costs are 10+19+10+13 = 52, six less than never pitting. Query the policy at the state actually reached, rather than reading one fixed column of actions across incompatible ages.

In reward notation the optimal total is −52 and the worse strategy is −58. A larger negative reward is preferable; a smaller positive cost is preferable. There is no contradiction between the two orderings.

Recap and self-check

Backward induction solves a finite horizon by using the boundary and propagating consequences earlier. It sees benefits that one-step greed misses.

At lap three, age two, must the solver choose STAY?

Both actions attain cost 29. We document STAY as the tie rule. A different tie rule can produce another optimal action without changing the value.
