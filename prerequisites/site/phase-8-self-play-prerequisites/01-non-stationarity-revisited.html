When the Opponent Learns Too

Phase 6 page 07 explained why scripted rivals keep the environment still. Phase 8 removes that on purpose.

Your call · copies chasing copies

The pit-timing duel, blocking on: two equal cars, the follower 0.5 s behind, one stop each. Each round the follower picks the best reply to the leader's last stop, then the leader picks the best reply to the follower's new one. Both start on lap 8.

Where does this self-play settle?

The assumption that held until now

Everything Phases 4 and 5 rely on assumes a stationary environment: the same state, visited at two different times, gives the same distribution over next states and rewards. Phase 6's rivals follow a pit_laps plan with no learning of their own, so they are part of a fixed environment, and the convergence arguments keep applying.

What breaks once the opponent learns

In Phase 8 the opponent's policy is part of the environment, and it changes as training goes on. A stop under the safety car that worked against last week's opponent may fail against this week's, with nothing changed about the track or the tyres. The transitions the learner is trying to learn move under it.

The replay memory gets a second kind of staleness too. Phase 5 used it to break the correlation between consecutive laps. In self-play a stored lap was played against an opponent that may no longer be in the pool: replaying it trains against a game that no longer exists. That doesn't rule replay out, and page 03's pool is part of how the plan manages it.

The numbers sayA cycle of three

From (8, 8): the follower answers lap 8 with lap 6, the leader covers with lap 7; the follower answers 7 with 5, the leader goes long to 10; the follower answers 10 with 9, the leader comes back to 8; and the follower answers 8 with 6 again. (6, 7) → (5, 10) → (9, 8) → (6, 7), for ever. Each copy chases what the other did last round.

With blocking off it settles at once: lap 8 is each car's best stop whatever the other does, because neither can cost the other time. The cycle exists because the cars can hold each other up. Below, you are the follower against a leader that plays this way. It is predictable, so it can be beaten.

Three ways to organize multi-agent training

  • Independent learners. Each agent has its own network, memory and weights, and learns from its own experience; every other agent is part of its (now changing) environment. The simplest setup, and the plan's.
  • Parameter sharing. One network for every agent, trained on everyone's experience. It learns faster, and assumes the agents are interchangeable, which every car here is. The plan doesn't use it.
  • Centralized training, decentralized execution. A shared critic sees the joint state during training; each agent acts on its own observation. More powerful (it can represent how one agent's action affects another's return) and more complex; MADDPG is built this way.

Independent learners is a choice, not a corner cut. Phase 5's DuelingDoubleDQNAgent already works; the plan reuses it unmodified, one per car, and expects the undercut and the overcut to come from the race's payoffs rather than a shared critic.

Evidence so farA learning opponent makes the target move.
