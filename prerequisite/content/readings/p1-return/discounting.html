Why this matters

An ordinary sum values equal rewards equally regardless of when they arrive. Some objectives prefer earlier benefits or need a finite measure of an indefinitely continuing task. A discount factor changes the weight of rewards according to their distance from the decision being evaluated.

In the browser lab, a broken rule multiplies every reward by 0.5 once. On this particular list it accidentally also gives 2.5. That coincidence does not make the rule correct. Test gamma 0.25: the correct first return is 2 − 0.25 + 0.0625 × 4 = 2; the broken rule gives 1.25. Comparing only one total can hide a wrong mechanism.

Read the compact notation

G_t = Σ_(k=0)^(T−t−1) γ^k r_(t+k)

The capital sigma, Σ, means add one term for each listed k. The index k counts how far the reward lies after t. γ^k means gamma multiplied by itself k times; at k=0 that weight is 1, including when gamma is 0. Thus gamma 0 keeps only the immediate reward. Gamma 1 gives every reward weight 1, recovering the finite sum from the previous reading.

Gamma is dimensionless. It does not convert a score in seconds into a percentage, and it is not the learning rate used to adjust an estimate. We have not introduced a learning update at all; we are defining the objective being estimated.

Discounting can change the preferred behavior

Consider two terminating options. EARLY gives 3 immediately. LATE gives 0 now and 4 one transition later. With gamma 1 their returns are 3 and 4, so LATE wins. With gamma 0.5 they are 3 and 2, so EARLY wins. Discount is not just a device for making numbers smaller: it can change the decision problem.

Common mistake: discounting the immediate reward once, or keeping its old absolute time index when scoring from a later starting point. Distances are measured from the return's own starting decision.

At gamma 0.5, why is the return from the last transition 4 rather than 1?

There is no delay from that starting point. Its first reward has weight 1. The weight 0.25 applied only when scoring that same reward from two transitions earlier.
