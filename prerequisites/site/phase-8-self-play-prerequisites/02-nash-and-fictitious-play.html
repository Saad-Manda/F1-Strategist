Phase 8 · Page 02 · Nash and fictitious play

The Game Underneath

Self-play isn't chasing "the optimal policy". It is chasing a solution to a two-player game, a relative notion of solved.

Your call · matching pennies

Matching pennies: each player shows heads or tails; the row player wins 1 on a match and loses 1 otherwise. Best-replying to the opponent's last move cycles for ever. Instead, each player best-replies to the opponent's average play so far.

After 2,000 rounds, how often does each player show heads?

Why "optimal policy" stops being the target

Through Phase 6 the agent wanted the best policy against a fixed environment: one best answer. In self-play each player picks a whole pit-timing policy, and the return of each depends on both. That is a two-player game, and "optimal" has nothing fixed to be optimal against.

Nash equilibrium

The natural notion of solved: a pair of strategies where neither player can do better by switching alone.

(π_A*, π_B*) is a Nash equilibrium if Return_A(π_A*, π_B*) ≥ Return_A(π_A, π_B*) for every π_A Return_B(π_A*, π_B*) ≥ Return_B(π_A*, π_B) for every π_B

Each player's best depends on the other's, so the two conditions hold together. In matching pennies no pure pair satisfies them: at any of the four cells, one player gains 2 by switching. The equilibrium is a mix, each side 50/50, where heads and tails both earn 0 and neither player can gain by moving.

Fictitious play

A classical way to find it: each round, each player best-replies to the average of the other's play so far, not to its last move. In two-player zero-sum games this provably converges to an equilibrium. The checkpoint pool (page 03) is the plan's practical stand-in for it.

Each player still swings, in longer and longer runs of one move, but the averages close in: 0.5002 heads and 0.4998 tails for both after 2,000 rounds. Best-replying to history rather than to the latest move is what turns the cycle into convergence.

The duel does the same. Fictitious play's exploitability, how much both players together could gain by switching to a best reply, falls from 0.140 after 10 rounds to 0.006 after 1,000. Round 1: col so far 100% H → row H. row so far 2 H, 0 T → col T Round 2: col so far 1 H, 1 T (tie) → row H. row so far 3 H, 0 T → col T Round 3: col so far 1 H, 2 T → row T. row so far 3 H, 1 T → col T Round 4: col so far 1 H, 3 T → row T. row so far 3 H, 2 T → col T Round 5: col so far 1 H, 4 T → row T. row so far 3 H, 3 T → col H

An earlier version of this page used a two-move rock-paper game. It can't cycle: paper beats or ties rock against either move, so best replies go to (paper, paper) and stay. Cycling needs a game where every move has an answer, as in matching pennies, rock-paper-scissors and the duel. Lab p8-02 runs all three.

Deep divePopulation-based training, in one paragraph60 words

AlphaStar, named in the plan's own docstring, is the large-scale descendant of this idea: keep a population of diverse policies and train against a mixture of them. That approximates fictitious play's averaging and adds robustness against over-fitting one opponent's style. It is why the plan keeps up to 50 checkpoints rather than training against the latest only.

Warm-up, in the page · checking an equilibrium by hand

A pair of strategies is a Nash equilibrium when neither player gains by switching on their own. In matching pennies the row player wins 1 on a match and loses 1 on a mismatch; the column player gets the opposite.

Now the game this page used to use: two moves, R and P. Paper beats rock; the same move ties. As the row player: R against R is 0, R against P is −1, P against R is +1, P against P is 0.

What is the equilibrium of the R/P game?
Worked solution for the warm-up

Matching pennies: at (H, H) the column player gains 2 by switching, so no pure pair is stable; against 50/50 both actions earn 0, so 50/50 is. Rock-paper-scissors: every move earns 0 against 1/3 each. The R/P game: P dominates R, so the equilibrium is pure, (P, P).
