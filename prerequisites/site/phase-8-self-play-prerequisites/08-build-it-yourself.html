Build It Yourself: The Pit-Timing Duel

Two equal cars, one stop each, and the only question is who stops first. Seven stages: the payoff, best responses and the loop that cycles, fictitious play, a checkpoint pool of best responses, the plan's own pool rule, the pit-delta histogram, and page 07's null test on it.

This page is the brief for the capstone lab below. It replaces an earlier toy built on a two-move rock-paper game. That game has a dominant move (paper ties paper and beats rock), so its equilibrium is pure and nothing in it could cycle or need a pool. The game here has neither problem, and it is the plan's own question in miniature.

The duel

The two-car race of Phase 6's capstone (labkit.duel: wake, being held up, passing on the dice), with blocking as a switch. Two changes make a real undercut possible: tyres that wear faster (0.25 s per lap of age on A, 0.15 s on B) and a smaller out-lap warm-up (0.3 s). With the toy race's own tyres, fresh B plus the 1.2 s warm-up is slower than worn A, and stopping first never gains anything. The cars are equally fast; the follower starts 0.5 s behind the leader. Each stops once, onto B, on a lap from 2 to 18.

A strategy is a stop lap, so the game is a 17 × 17 matrix. The follower's payoff is +1 for finishing ahead and −1 if not, plus 0.05 per second of final margin; the leader's is the negative. The follower is the row player and maximises; the leader is the column player and minimises. Each entry is averaged over 20 seeds of pass dice, the same seeds in every cell. labkit.pitgame.matrix(interaction) holds both matrices, cached. Every stage below is exact: no stage trains anything, so none of them is noisy.

Stage 2 — best responses, and self-play against the latest snapshot

A best response to a mixture is an argmax (row) or argmin (column) of a matrix-vector product, page 02's definition. latest_only is naive self-play: each round the follower best-responds to the leader's last lap, then the leader to the follower's new one.

  • Blocking off: lap 8 is each car's best reply to every lap the other could choose. The cars cannot cost each other time, so each runs its own solo race. Latest-only sits at (8, 8) from the first round: a pure equilibrium.
  • Blocking on: no pure equilibrium. The best the follower can guarantee with one lap is −1.075; the best the leader can hold it to is −0.398, a saddle gap of 0.68. Latest-only cycles with period 3: (6, 7) → (5, 10) → (9, 8) → (6, 7). The follower undercuts, the leader covers by stopping later, the follower overcuts, the leader stops earlier, and round it goes. This is page 01's chasing, with laps.
Reference snippet (a last-resort hint: try the stage first) 
def best_response(U, opp_mix, player):
    if player == "row":
        return int(np.argmax(U @ opp_mix))
    return int(np.argmin(opp_mix @ U))

i, j = start
out = []
for _ in range(rounds):
    i = best_response(U, pure(j), "row")
    j = best_response(U, pure(i), "col")
    out.append((i, j))

Stage 3 — fictitious play

Respond to the opponent's average play instead of its last move, follower first, then the leader against the follower's updated counts. Exploitability, the total that both players together could gain by switching to a best response, falls from 0.140 after 10 rounds to 0.080 after 100, 0.006 after 1,000 and 0.002 after 10,000. The equilibrium the duel converges to:

  • The follower mixes undercuts and an overcut: lap 4 (11%), 5 (45%), 6 (20%) or 9 (24%).
  • The leader covers: lap 5 (19%), 6 (14%), 7 (36%) or 8 (32%).
  • The follower's expected payoff is −0.77: it usually still loses, but by less than the −1.075 of copying the leader.
Reference snippet (a last-resort hint: try the stage first) 
cr, cc = pure(start[0]), pure(start[1])
trace = []

    cc[best_response(U, cr / cr.sum(), "col")] += 1

Stage 4 — a checkpoint pool of best responses

Page 03's pool, with laps in place of networks: each snapshot is one pure strategy. pool_mix turns a pool into the mixture that sampling from it gives, with page 03's recency rule (and the n = 1 guard, lab p8-03). pool_play is self-play against the pool: each round each player adds a best response to the other's pool mixture. Two checks tie the stages together:

  • Uniform sampling with no window is fictitious play. The pool's mixture is the frequency of past play, so the two traces agree to rounding (the build measured the difference at 3 × 10⁻¹⁵).
  • A window of one is latest-only. max_size=1 keeps only the newest snapshot, and stage 2's cycle comes back.
Reference snippet (a last-resort hint: try the stage first) 
n = len(pool)
if recency_bias is None or n == 1:
    w = np.full(n, 1.0 / n)
else:
    cut = max(1, int(0.8 * n))
    w = np.where(np.arange(n) >= cut, recency_bias / (n - cut), (1 - recency_bias) / cut)
mix = np.zeros(N)
np.add.at(mix, np.asarray(pool), w)

Stage 5 — the plan's rule

No new code: run pool_play with the plan's settings, 70% from the newest 20% and at most 50 entries. It does not settle. Over the last 200 of 1,000 rounds its exploitability averages 0.38, against fictitious play's 0.006. Each piece of the rule costs something (the build's numbers, averaged over the last 200 rounds):

  • Recency with an unbounded window: 0.31.
  • Uniform sampling with a window of 50: 0.62.
  • Latest-only, over its cycle: 0.75, 2.26 and 1.04.

The window forgets the old strategies that the equilibrium mixes in, and the recency bias weights the newest ones, which are exactly the ones still chasing. The plan's pool sits between the two extremes, much nearer the cycle than the equilibrium. That is a measurement on this game, not a verdict on the plan: with networks that generalise, the trade-off between forgetting and adapting is different. But it is not free.

Stage 6 — the histogram the plan wants to read

delta_distribution: the distribution of (follower's stop lap − leader's) when both draw from their mixtures. At the stage 3 equilibrium:

That is the plan's "undercut and overcut" histogram, produced by the game itself, with blocking as the whole difference (page 06).

Stage 7 — your null on your result

Draw 2,000 races from the stage 3 mixtures and apply page 07's shuffle test. It does not reject (p = 0.93), and it is right not to. Each car chose its lap before the start, so neither reacted to the other within a race, and the shuffle keeps each car's own distribution, which is where the shape lives. A histogram can be strategic all the way through and still pass the shuffle test. "Did they react?" and "is the shape strategic?" are different questions. The second is answered by comparing with blocking off, as stage 6 did.

Reference snippet (a last-resort hint: try the stage first) 
real = float(np.mean(f_laps == l_laps))
null = np.array([np.mean(f_laps == rng.permutation(l_laps)) for _ in range(shuffles)])
far = np.abs(null - null.mean()) >= abs(real - null.mean())
return float((far.sum() + 1) / (shuffles + 1))

The report

The lab's report notebook, about six minutes of given cells, does what the plan does: it trains learners by self-play on the same duel, tabular ones that choose every lap and see whether each car has stopped. It runs sixteen of them: latest-only or a recency pool, blocking on or off, four seeds each. It draws each run's histogram against the exact equilibrium, and the spread is the result. With blocking on, the pool learners' follower-first share ran from 0.09 to 0.78 across four seeds (exact: 0.61). With blocking off, where the exact answer is all at 0, learners still put up to 1.00 on one side. These learners can react within a race, so the shuffle can reject them, and it did for three of four blocking-on runs. It also rejected one blocking-off run: a learner that covers its rival's stop is reacting whether or not the reaction pays. One run's histogram is weak evidence (page 10).
