Prioritized Experience Replay: Not All Transitions Are Equally Worth Replaying

Pit stops decide the race and make up about 3% of the data. Uniform sampling shows them to the network about 3% of the time. Prioritized replay samples by how wrong the network was, then corrects for having done so.

How rare the decisive transitions are

Page 04's buffer samples every stored transition with equal probability. Put this project's numbers into that:

laps per race: 52 pit stops per race, average: about 1.5 share of steps that are pits: 1.5 / 52 ≈ 2.9% about 1 step in 35 a uniform batch of 64 contains, on average: 64 × 0.029 ≈ 1.8 pit transitions and about 62 "stay out" transitions

The 62 look much alike: one more lap on slightly older tyres. The network learns to predict those well quite early. The two that carry the strategy are outvoted in every batch.

TD error as a measure of surprise

Phase 4 page 01 introduced the TD error δ = target − Q(s,a) as the size of an update. It can be read a second way: how surprised the network was by this transition. A transition with a small |δ| is one the network already predicts well, and replaying it teaches little. A transition with a large |δ| is one it got badly wrong. Prioritized experience replay (PER) uses |δ| as a sampling signal, so the buffer hands back more of what the network currently gets wrong.

From priority to probability

Each stored transition i gets a priority and a sampling probability:

p_i = |δ_i| + ε ε is a tiny constant, so no priority is ever exactly 0 P(i) = p_i^α / Σ_k p_k^α each priority raised to α, divided by the sum over the buffer

The exponent α sets how much the priorities count. At α = 0 every p_i^α is 1 and sampling is uniform again. At α = 1 probability is directly proportional to error. The real plan uses α = 0.6, in between.

Work it for a buffer of four transitions: three routine laps the network predicts to within half a second, and one pit stop it got wrong by eight seconds.

The α = 0.6 row, step by step: 0.5^0.6 = 0.660 8.0^0.6 = 3.482 sum = 3 × 0.660 + 3.482 = 5.461 P(routine) = 0.660 / 5.461 = 12.1% P(pit) = 3.482 / 5.461 = 63.8%

The error was 16 times larger, and at α = 0.6 the pit stop is sampled about 5.3 times as often as a routine lap (3.482 / 0.660). The exponent softens the ratio on purpose, for two reasons.

  • Priorities go stale. A transition's |δ| is only recomputed when that transition is sampled. A transition that was unsurprising long ago keeps its low priority even if the network has since changed and would now get it wrong. Sampling by error alone would starve it for good.
  • Diversity. Training on the same few high-error transitions again and again fits those few and forgets the rest, which is page 04's correlated-data problem coming back in another form.

One more rule follows from stale priorities. A new transition has no δ yet, because it has never been through the network. It is stored at the largest priority currently in the buffer, so that it is sampled at least once and gets a real priority.

The correction: importance-sampling weights

Sampling by priority changes what the network is trained on. If pit stops are 3% of experience and 15% of training batches, the network is fitting a world in which pit stops are five times more common than they are. Its estimates drift toward that distorted world. The fix is to shrink the loss of each sampled transition by how over-represented it was:

w_i = ( N · P(i) )^(−β) N is the number of transitions in the buffer then divide every w_i in the batch by the largest one, so that weights only ever scale a loss down

N · P(i) is how many times more likely transition i was to be drawn than under uniform sampling. Under uniform sampling it is exactly 1. Continue the four-transition example at α = 0.6:

At β = 1 the correction is complete: the pit stop is drawn 5.3 times as often and each draw counts 0.189 as much, and 5.3 × 0.189 = 1.0. At β = 0.4 the correction is partial: 5.3 × 0.514 ≈ 2.7, so the pit stop still has 2.7 times the influence of a routine lap.

That raises a fair question. If full correction cancels the extra sampling, what was gained? Two things. First, the pit stop's influence now arrives as several small steps spread over many batches, where uniform sampling would deliver it as one large step on the rare occasion it is drawn. Many small steps are much easier for gradient descent to absorb. Second, β is not held at 1. It starts at 0.4 and is raised toward 1 over training. Early on, estimates are poor everywhere and extra attention on surprising transitions is worth a little distortion. Late in training, the network is settling on final values and the distortion matters, so it is corrected away.

Where the priorities come from during training

Each training step does four things with the buffer, in this order: sample a batch by priority, compute each transition's TD error as part of the loss, multiply each transition's loss by its weight w_i, then write the new |δ| values back as those transitions' priorities. The write-back is what keeps the buffer honest. Once the network has learned a pit stop, its |δ| falls, and it stops being sampled so often.

The sum tree

Sampling "in proportion to priority" from 100,000 transitions has an obvious method: add up priorities from the start until the running total passes a random number. That costs up to 100,000 additions per sample, and updating a priority means recomputing the total. A sum tree does both in about 17 steps, the depth of a binary tree with 100,000 leaves.

Every leaf holds one transition's p^α. Every node above holds the sum of its two children, so the root holds the total. A four-leaf tree with leaf values 3, 1, 4, 2:

[10] root = total / \ [4] [6] each node = sum of its two children / \ / \ [3] [1] [4] [2] leaves, one per stored transition leaf0 leaf1 leaf2 leaf3

Drawing a sample. Pick a random number between 0 and the root's total, say 6.5, and walk down. At each node, go left if the number is no larger than the left child's value. Otherwise subtract the left child's value and go right.

at root: left child = 4. 6.5 > 4 → subtract: 6.5 − 4 = 2.5, go right at node [6]: left child = 4. 2.5 ≤ 4 → go left arrived at leaf2 (value 4) Leaf2 owns the stretch from 4 to 8 of the total 10, so it is reached 4 times in 10. That is exactly "probability proportional to its value".

Updating a priority. Change leaf1 from 1 to 5. The change is +4. Add it to every node on the path from that leaf up to the root, and touch nothing else:

leaf1: 1 → 5 its parent: 4 → 8 root: 10 → 14

The real buffer stores the tree in one flat array, with the root at index 0, the children of node k at 2k+1 and 2k+2, and the leaves in the last capacity slots. That is why its code converts between a transition's index and a tree index by adding or subtracting capacity − 1. It also draws a batch by cutting the total into as many equal segments as the batch has slots and drawing one number from each, which spreads a batch across the whole range of priorities.

A bug to watch for, recorded against the real plan

The leaves hold p^α, not p. The plan's add() reads the largest leaf to find "the current maximum priority", then raises that number to α a second time before storing it. With a largest leaf of 3.482 (the pit stop above), a new transition should also be stored at 3.482. The buggy version stores 3.482^0.6 = 2.114, about 40% too low, so new transitions do not get the "at least once, soon" treatment they were promised. Page 09 has you reproduce this in your own buffer, and page 10 places it among the plan's recorded issues.
