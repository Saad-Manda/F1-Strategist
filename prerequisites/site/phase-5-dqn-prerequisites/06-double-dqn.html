Double DQN: A Bias Vanilla DQN Doesn't Know It Has

The max of several uncertain numbers is too high on average, and vanilla DQN takes one inside every target. The fix changes which network chooses and which one scores.

Your call · the max of three guesses

Three next calls are each worth 5. The network hasn't converged: each estimate is 4 or 6 with equal odds, independently. The target takes the max of the three.

On average, what does the max report?

Why a max over noisy estimates runs high

Page 03 left the target as r + γ·max_a′ Q(s′, a′; θ⁻). The max does two jobs with one network: the target network chooses the best-looking next call, and the same network scores it. Each estimate has some error in it, and the max picks whichever landed high and reports that lucky number as the value. That is maximization bias. Phase 4's table had it too; it matters more here because a network's errors are larger and last longer, and the real network scores six calls at once.

The inflated number doesn't stay put. It becomes part of the target for the state before, whose estimate feeds the state before that, and with γ = 1 over 52 laps it adds up. It is also largest where estimates are least certain: the calls rarely tried. In this project those are the pit stops.

The reveal · the max

You calledno call made
The numbers say5.75, a bias of +0.75

Three estimates of 4 or 6 make 8 equally likely cases. The max is 4 only when all three came out low, 1 case in 8; otherwise it is 6. So E[max] = (1/8)(4) + (7/8)(6) = 5.75, against a true best of 5.

No single estimate is biased. The bias comes from the max, which always picks whichever estimate happened to land high.

The fix: one network chooses, another scores

Keep two estimates with independent errors. Let A choose the call and read its value from B. B's estimate of A's choice is 4 or 6 with equal odds, because B's error has nothing to do with A's, so E[B(a*)] = 5.00. A still tends to choose a call it overrates; that no longer matters, because A's number is never used as the value. Double DQN does this with the two networks the agent already has:

Vanilla DQNDouble DQN
Choose the next calla* = argmax_a′ Q(s′,a′;θ⁻)a* = argmax_a′ Q(s′,a′;θ)
Score itQ(s′,a*;θ⁻)Q(s′,a*;θ⁻)

Only the weights inside the argmax differ. In code it is three lines: run the online network on s′, take its argmax, read that one value from the target network's output. That is the plan's "3-line change". The two networks share most of their errors, since page 03's target is a slow copy of the online one, so Double DQN reduces the bias and doesn't remove it. It can tip slightly the other way, and a small error either way does less harm than a steady push upward that feeds on itself.

Masking inside the choice, and the terminal cut-off

The online network outputs a number for every call, legal or not, and a number for a call that never happens is never corrected. So illegal next calls are set to −∞ before the argmax, which is why each stored transition carries the next state's legal-call mask: it is needed when the transition is replayed, long after the race has moved on. And the last lap has no next state, so its target is r alone. On a batch that is written as arithmetic, target = r + γ·Q(s′, a*; θ⁻)·(1 − done). Leave the factor out and the network invents a "value after the race" that leaks backward into every lap; the capstone report plants that fault on page 09.

Each row's loss compares its target with Q(s, a; θ) for the call actually taken. Picking "the column for the call taken, row by row" out of the network's output is PyTorch's gather, and the same operation reads Q(s′, a*; θ⁻) from the target network's. Page 10's two gather calls are these two.

Deep diveThe deadly triad, and further reading110 words

Three ingredients are now used together: a function approximator, bootstrapping, and off-policy learning (the max, and replaying old data). Sutton and Barto call this the deadly triad: any two are safe, and all three can make value estimates grow without limit. Phase 4's table had the last two and was safe. The target network, the replay memory and Double DQN remove no ingredient; each weakens a way the three reinforce one another.

Optional: Hado van Hasselt, Arthur Guez and David Silver, Deep Reinforcement Learning with Double Q-learning (2015). Sutton and Barto, Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction (2nd edition), section 6.7 on maximization bias and section 11.3 on the triad.

The prime suspect

A network that overrates a rarely tried pit stop is what this page warns of, and it happens. The same learner trained with seed 0, the same 2,000 races, boxes for B on lap 8 and then for A on lap 16 in every green race: 3.29 s from the optimum. On lap 16 it rates the stop 0.95 s too well and staying out 2.35 s too badly, enough to favour the stop by 0.25 s when the truth is 3.05 s the other way. That is the signature: one state, every race, a wrong number in the network.

The champion doesn't fit it. Its greedy strategy makes one stop, the oracle's, and its unplanned stops land on different laps in different races, at states where its numbers favour staying out by seconds.
