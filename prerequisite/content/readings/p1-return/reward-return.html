Why this matters

In the delivery chain, the final RIGHT move gave +5 while the two-move episode totaled +4. A reward describes one transition. A return combines rewards from a starting decision onward. Comparing policies by just their latest reward can ignore the costs needed to reach that reward.

Start with an ordinary sum

Suppose a different delivery task gives rewards [2, -1, 4] on three successive transitions and then terminates. The first return is 2 − 1 + 4 = 5. Starting at the second transition leaves −1 + 4 = 3; starting at the last leaves 4. These three returns describe three different starting points on the same trajectory.

A trajectory is the sequence of states, actions and outcomes produced by a run. Its rewards are evidence about that run, not the average outcome of every possible run. If your first decision changes the future route, its consequences include the later rewards on the route it creates.

For the original chain, RIGHT, RIGHT gives [-1, 5], total 4. LEFT, RIGHT, RIGHT gives [-1, -1, 5], total 3. Both reach the goal and receive the same final reward; the unnecessary move makes the second sequence worse under this cumulative objective.

What is the reward trying to express?

The reward hypothesis is a modeling idea: describe desired behavior through maximizing the expected cumulative value of a scalar reward. “Scalar” means one number. It does not prove that a chosen proxy captures everything we care about, or that all goals are easy to represent with a useful signal.

Imagine paying the delivery robot +1 every time it visits the parcel shelf. A robot could repeatedly leave and revisit the shelf without delivering anything. It has optimized the stated reward while failing the intended task. More training against that reward can make the mismatch worse.

Define the actual objective before adding convenient bonuses. If the goal is prompt delivery, a terminal delivery reward and a cost per move make unnecessary movement costly. If battery damage or safety also matters, explicitly decide how those consequences enter the objective or constraints. A legal-action mask and a reward penalty have different roles.

Write the finite return

Let t identify the first transition we want to score, and T the number of transitions before true termination. We use r_t for the reward returned on transition t. Some textbooks call the same returned reward R_(t+1); this is a naming convention, not a different calculation.

G_t = r_t + r_(t+1) + … + r_(T−1)

The dots mean “continue adding the intervening rewards.” G_t is the return from t. At T there are no task rewards left, so G_T = 0. The last real reward stays in the sum; the zero tail does not erase it.

Common mistake: storing this accumulated total in every trace row's reward field. Preserve each step reward and compute returns separately.

The rewards are [2, −1, 4]. Is the last step's reward 5?

No. It is 4. Five is the undiscounted return starting at the first transition; three is the return starting at the second.
