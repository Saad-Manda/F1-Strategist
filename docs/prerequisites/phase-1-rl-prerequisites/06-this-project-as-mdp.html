Page 06 / 08 — Grounding

This Project, Formalized as an MDP

Pages 1–5, inverted: every formal object from page 2's tuple, matched to exactly what it is in this project's actual design.

The state space, S

The observation is a 16-dimensional vector, entirely in [0, 1]:

IndexFeatureEncoding
0Laps remainingNormalized fraction of the race left
1–5Tyre compoundOne-hot: SOFT, MEDIUM, HARD, INTERMEDIATE, WET
6Tyre ageNormalized (capped at an assumed max age of 50 laps)
7–9Weather regimeOne-hot: DRY, DAMP, WET
10Track temperatureRescaled from a 15–65 °C range
11Safety car activeBinary
12–14Compounds usedBitmask over SOFT/MEDIUM/HARD
15Mandatory pit neededBinary — derived from 12–14 and the two-compound rule

Every feature here is either directly observable in a real race broadcast or trivially derivable from it — a deliberate modelling choice: the agent gets exactly the information a strategist actually has, no more (it never sees the exact calibration constants) and no less.

The action space, A

Six discrete choices: STAY_OUT, or pit for one of SOFT, MEDIUM, HARD, INTERMEDIATE, WET. Not all six are legal in every state — see the action mask below.

The transition function, P

dynamics.py is where P lives, and by the time you're reading it, none of its shape should be a surprise — page 5 built exactly this transition function, term by term, in a toy of your own: a pace term per compound, a degradation term with a cliff, a fuel term, a weather-mismatch term, a warmup term, all summed into one lap time, with an explicit escape hatch for a fixed, calibrated time on safety-car and VSC laps where nobody's choices affect the outcome. What's different in the real file is only scale and precision — real calibrated constants instead of illustrative ones, five real compounds instead of a toy's two or three, a proper weather-mismatch table instead of a single penalty — not structure. If any piece of that composition feels unfamiliar, that's the signal to go back to page 5 and build the missing piece as a toy before reading the real file.

The tyre-age, weather-regime, and compounds-used bookkeeping that turns one state into the next lives in F1StrategyEnv.step(), and it embeds the same ordering rule page 5's pit-stop stage built by hand: a pit stop is charged against the lap you're currently finishing, on the tyre you started that lap on — the new tyre (and its warmup penalty) only takes effect from the next lap. Getting this order backward would let a pit stop simultaneously avoid a lap of degradation, dodge the warmup penalty, and gain fresh-tyre pace — three unearned advantages stacked on the exact lap where the model should be charging the agent the most, not the least.

Two further pieces of P are stochastic and sampled independently at every reset(): weather (ScenarioLoader, from real historical race traces) and safety cars (SafetyCarSampler, drawn fresh from a calibrated hazard rate every episode, deliberately not replayed from history — see page 1's note on reward hacking, and page 5's own hazard-sampling stage for why "fresh every episode" has to be true in code, not just in principle).

The reward function, R

R = -lap_time on every single lap, safety cars included, plus a -500 terminal backstop if the race ends without the mandatory two-dry-compound rule satisfied (structurally unreachable in normal play — see the mask section below). An optional reward_offset setting subtracts a constant (base_pace) from every step for numerical reasons (page 3) without changing which policy is optimal; info["lap_time"] always reports the true, uncentred value regardless of this setting.

The discount factor, γ

γ = 1.0. Justified on pages 2 and 3: episodes are guaranteed fixed-length, so the return sum is always finite regardless of γ, and γ = 1 is the only choice under which return = -total_race_time exactly, with no implicit bias toward early- or late-race pace.

The action mask

compute_action_mask encodes five rules as a single boolean array, shared verbatim between the environment and the Phase 3.5 DP oracle:

  1. Slick compounds (SOFT/MEDIUM/HARD) are only legal in DRY or DAMP.
  2. INTERMEDIATE is only legal in DAMP or WET.
  3. WET is only legal in WET.
  4. You cannot "pit" for the compound you're already on.
  5. STAY_OUT is forced off if the two-compound rule isn't yet satisfied and only 2 laps remain — unless the race has been declared wet, which voids the rule entirely (the real regulation).

Rule 5 is why the -500 terminal penalty above should never actually fire during ordinary play: the mask makes the violation it punishes structurally impossible for any agent that respects action_masks(). The penalty exists purely as a backstop for consumers that bypass masking, and the Phase 1 test suite treats "the penalty never fires in normal play" as a success condition, not a gap in test coverage.

Two entries are worth noticing precisely because they're easy to get backward: lap_time_compound is MEDIUM, not HARD — it records what the just-completed lap was actually run on, which is the old tyre, per the ordering rule above. And action_masks describes what's legal next (lap 31, on HARD), not what was legal when the action was chosen — a consumer reading this for the state the action was taken in is reading the wrong lap's mask.

Why one shared implementation matters more here than usual

Nothing above is unusual RL design in isolation — every piece has a name and a textbook description on pages 1–5. What is specific to this project is the insistence that P and the action mask exist in exactly one place (dynamics.py), called identically by the environment, the baselines, and the DP oracle. A DP oracle that computes optimal Q-values against a subtly different transition function than the one agents actually train against produces numbers that look authoritative and are simply wrong — and because both implementations would look individually correct, this class of bug is close to invisible without the "single implementation, multiple callers" discipline the plan insists on.
