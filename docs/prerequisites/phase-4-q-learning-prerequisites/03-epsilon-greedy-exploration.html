Exploration: ε-Greedy

Nothing said so far explains how the agent decides what to actually do. ε-greedy is the simplest answer that works, and it resolves the exploration/exploitation tradeoff Phase 1 page 1 only named.

Why greedy-only fails

An agent that always takes whatever action its current Q-table says is best can get permanently stuck on a mediocre policy — not because the table is wrong in some abstract sense, but because it never tries the action that would have revealed something better. If an early, still-unreliable estimate happens to rank a mediocre pit lap above the genuinely optimal one, a purely greedy agent will keep confirming its own mistaken belief forever: it only ever revisits the action it already prefers, so the estimate for the better action never gets the chance to improve. This is the exploration/exploitation tradeoff named on Phase 1 page 1 — exploit what looks best so far, or explore to find out whether something else is actually better — resolved concretely here for the first time.

ε-greedy, defined

with probability ε: act uniformly at random, among LEGAL actions with probability 1 − ε: act greedily — argmax Q(s, ·), among LEGAL actions

"Among legal actions" matters in both branches, tying back to the action mask introduced in the Phase 1 and Phase 3.5 sets: a random draw has to be restricted to actions that are actually available in the current state, and the greedy argmax has to ignore illegal actions' Q-values entirely (masked out, typically by setting them to −∞ before taking the max) rather than accidentally selecting one just because its untrained Q-value happens to look high.

Linear decay, worked with the real plan's numbers

A fixed ε never stops exploring as much as it did on day one, which wastes training time once the agent already knows roughly what's good. The real plan decays ε linearly from ε_start = 1.0 (pure exploration at the very start of training) down to a floor ε_end = 0.05 (never fully greedy — always a small chance of trying something else) over decay_steps = 500,000 environment steps:

ε_t = max( ε_end, ε_start − t · (ε_start − ε_end) / decay_steps ) per-step decrement = (1.0 − 0.05) / 500,000 = 0.95 / 500,000 = 1.9 × 10⁻⁶

A race in this environment runs roughly 90 steps (one decision per lap, across a lap count in the 50s–90s depending on scenario). 500,000 steps at ~90 steps/episode is 500,000 / 90 ≈ 5,556 episodes before ε reaches its floor — a little over half of the real plan's 10,000-episode training run. That's a deliberate design point, not a coincidence: the second half of training happens almost entirely at the 5% exploration floor, giving the table time to refine an already-mostly-formed policy rather than continuing to explore at the same rate throughout.

Common pitfall

Decaying ε too fast locks in whatever policy the table happened to prefer early, before enough states have been visited to trust it — the exact same failure mode as the greedy-only case above, just delayed rather than avoided. If a trained agent's learning curve flattens early at a mediocre level, checking whether epsilon_decay_steps was too small relative to the total training budget is one of the first things worth trying, ahead of anything more exotic (this is also why the real plan's risk table names "increase epsilon_decay_steps to 1M" as a mitigation for premature convergence).

Why masking has to apply inside exploration, not just at evaluation time

It's tempting to think of the action mask as something that only matters for the greedy branch — after all, isn't a random exploratory action supposed to be random? The problem is that "random over all 6 actions" wastes exploration budget on moves that are illegal in the current state and get rejected or corrupt training, when only a handful of those 6 are actually legal here (say, only one valid pit compound is available because the mandatory-service rule restricts choices, or every pit action is illegal on the parade lap). Masking has to narrow the random draw itself to the legal set, not just filter the final choice afterward.

A concrete comparison makes the cost precise. Suppose 6 actions total, of which exactly 3 are legal in some state, and exactly one of those 3 is the useful pit action the agent needs to discover. Over 10 exploratory visits to that state, the chance of trying that one useful action at least once:

Exploration schemeP(pick the useful action), one drawP(tried at least once in 10 draws)
Unmasked — random over all 6 actions1/6 ≈ 0.1671 − (5/6)¹⁰ ≈ 0.838
Masked — random over the 3 legal actions1/3 ≈ 0.3331 − (2/3)¹⁰ ≈ 0.983

Both numbers look reasonably high over 10 draws, but the gap compounds badly at states that are visited rarely (exactly the coarse-table cells page 5 and 6 worry about) or where more than one of the 6 actions is illegal — a state with only 2 legal actions out of 6 more than doubles the masked scheme's per-draw hit rate over the unmasked one. Masking inside exploration isn't a correctness nicety; it's what makes the exploration budget actually land on the decisions the agent needs to learn.
