Phase 6 · Page 01 · One car to N

From One Car to N

Nothing about the agent's own tyres changes. What's new is an outcome that depends on cars it doesn't control.

Your call · three new numbers

The plan adds three numbers to the state: the gap to the car ahead, the gap to the car behind, and your position. The reward is still minus the lap time, in the plan's race.

How much can the best call depend on those three numbers?

What breaks, and what doesn't

Phase 1's state has 16 features, every one of them about the agent's own car: compound, tyre age, laps left, weather, safety car. Adding four more cars changes none of that. compute_lap_time is unchanged, and your tyres wear the way Phase 0's calibration says, whoever else is on track. What's new is a second question on top of the first. Driving your own race well is no longer the whole story, because there is now a relative outcome, position and the gap to the cars around you, that depends on choices the agent never makes.

Three new state features

The plan adds three: gap_to_car_ahead and gap_to_car_behind, in seconds and divided by a 60-second cap, and position_normalized, position / n_cars. The observation grows from 16 numbers to N_OBS_MULTI = N_OBS_SINGLE + N_NEW_FEATURES = 19.

Why these three, and not every rival's tyre age and compound? Phase 1 page 02's Markov argument again: the state needs what changes this lap's best call. Is a car close enough ahead to attack or close enough behind to threaten, and how is the race going overall? Rival number 3's tyres, four cars back, don't change what you should do, so they stay out, as total race time stayed out of Phase 1's state.

The reveal · what the numbers can change

The numbers sayNot at all, in the plan's race

With the reward minus the lap time, the best call is the one that makes the rest of your race fastest. In the plan's race each car's lap time comes from its own tyres, its own age and its own call. Nothing in it reads the gap or the position. So the three new numbers predict nothing about your future lap times, and the best call ignores them.

That doesn't make them useless: a reward that pays for position can still make them matter, and page 02 tries one. But it is the first sign of the case. The state now describes the rival; the race never asks about it.

A new network, not a reloaded one

The observation going from 16 to 19 numbers means DuelingDQN's first layer, sized for 16 inputs (Phase 5 page 05), has to be built for 19. A single-car checkpoint can't be reloaded for Phase 6; a fresh network is built with the new input width and trained from scratch. Better known now than met as a shape-mismatch error on training day.

Rivals as fixed scripts

The other cars are not RL agents. Each follows a pit plan, a starting compound and a list of (lap, new_compound) pairs, chosen before the race and never changed by anything the agent does. To the agent a scripted rival is one more given part of the environment, like the weather trace or the safety car. Page 07 explains the problem this sidesteps, a learning opponent, and why that is the right order before Phase 8.

The Pit Wall's rival is the labs' two-car race (labs/labkit/duel.py): the 20-lap toy race with the safety car off, a rival 0.3 s a lap slower starting 0.3 s ahead, and a script with one stop for B on lap 9. The leader flag isn't in the plan; page 06 shows why it is needed.

3 · Two consequences

Why can a Phase 5 checkpoint not simply be loaded into the 19-input network?
Four rivals with fixed pit-stop scripts are added. From the agent's point of view, is the environment still stationary?
Worked solution

Gap ahead 2.7 s → 0.045; gap behind 18.4 s → 0.307; position 2/3 = 0.667. Leading gives min(99/60, 1) = 1.0, the same as any gap of 60 s or more, e.g. 75 s behind. The checkpoint's first layer has 16 input columns. Fixed scripts keep the environment stationary.
