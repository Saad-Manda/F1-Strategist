Build It Yourself: the Pit-Timing Duel

Two equal cars, one stop each, and one question: who stops first? Seven exact stages, from the payoff to page 07's null on your own result.

Your call · both on lap 8

The follower starts 0.5 s behind an equal leader. Its payoff is +1 for finishing ahead, −1 if not, plus 0.05 per second of final margin. Blocking on, both stop on lap 8.

What is the follower's payoff?

The duel

The two-car race of Phase 6's capstone (labkit.duel: wake, being held up, passing on the dice), blocking as a switch, with two changes that make a real undercut possible: tyres that wear faster (0.25 s per lap of age on A, 0.15 s on B) and a 0.3 s out-lap warm-up. With the toy race's own tyres, fresh B plus the 1.2 s warm-up is slower than worn A, and stopping first never gains anything. Each car stops once, onto B, on a lap from 2 to 18, so the game is a 17 × 17 matrix: the follower is the row player and maximises, the leader minimises. Each entry is averaged over 20 seeds of pass dice, the same seeds in every cell. labkit.pitgame.matrix(interaction) holds both matrices; the Pit Wall's Duel mode ships them. No stage trains anything, so none is noisy.

The reveal · both on lap 8

You calledno call made
The numbers say−1.075

Stopping together keeps the order, and the follower spends the race in the leader's wake: it finishes behind, 1.5 s back. −1 − 0.05 × 1.5 = −1.075. With blocking off it stays 0.5 s back and gets −1.025.

−1.075 is also the best the follower can guarantee with a single lap against a leader who knows it. The best the leader can hold it to with one lap is −0.398. The 0.68 between them is why there is no pure equilibrium.

Stage 2: best replies, and self-play against the latest

A best reply to a mixture is an argmax (row) or argmin (column) of a matrix-vector product. latest_only is naive self-play. With blocking off, lap 8 is each car's best reply to every lap, and it sits at (8, 8) from the first round. With blocking on it cycles with period 3, (6, 7) → (5, 10) → (9, 8), page 01's chase with laps.

Reference snippet (a last-resort hint: try the stage first) 
def best_response(U, opp_mix, player):
    if player == "row":
        return int(np.argmax(U @ opp_mix))
    return int(np.argmin(opp_mix @ U))

i, j = start
out = []
for _ in range(rounds):
    i = best_response(U, pure(j), "row")
    j = best_response(U, pure(i), "col")
    out.append((i, j))

Stage 3: fictitious play

Reply to the opponent's average instead. Exploitability falls from 0.140 after 10 rounds to 0.080 after 100, 0.006 after 1,000 and 0.002 after 10,000. The follower mixes laps 4 (11%), 5 (45%), 6 (20%) and 9 (24%); the leader covers with 5 (19%), 6 (14%), 7 (36%) and 8 (32%). The follower's expected payoff is −0.77: it usually still loses, but by less than the −1.075 of copying the leader.

Stage 4: a pool of best replies

Page 03's pool, with laps for networks: pool_mix turns a pool into the mixture sampling from it gives (with the n = 1 guard), and pool_play adds a best reply to the other's pool mixture each round. Uniform sampling with no window is fictitious play (the two traces agree to 3 × 10⁻¹⁵); a window of one is latest-only, and stage 2's cycle comes back.

Stage 5: the plan's rule

No new code: pool_play with 70% from the newest 20% and at most 50 entries. Over the last 200 of 1,000 rounds its exploitability averages 0.38, against fictitious play's 0.006. Recency with no window alone gives 0.31; a window of 50 with uniform sampling alone, 0.62; latest-only, 0.75 to 2.26 over its cycle.

Stage 7: your null on your result

Draw 2,000 races from the equilibrium mixtures and apply page 07's shuffle. It doesn't reject (p = 0.93), and it is right not to: each car chose before the start, and the shuffle keeps each car's own distribution, where the shape lives.

Reference snippet (a last-resort hint: try the stage first) 
real = float(np.mean(f_laps == l_laps))
null = np.array([np.mean(f_laps == rng.permutation(l_laps)) for _ in range(shuffles)])
far = np.abs(null - null.mean()) >= abs(real - null.mean())
return float((far.sum() + 1) / (shuffles + 1))
Deep diveThe report: sixteen learners130 words

The report notebook, about six minutes of given cells, does what the plan does: it trains tabular self-play learners that choose every lap and see whether each car has stopped. Sixteen runs: latest-only or a recency pool, blocking on or off, four seeds each. With blocking on, the pool learners' follower-first share ran from 0.09 to 0.78 across four seeds (exact: 0.61). With blocking off, where the exact answer is all at 0, learners still put up to 1.00 on one side, from imperfect learning, not strategy. These learners can react within a race, so the shuffle can reject them, and it did for three of four blocking-on runs. It also rejected one blocking-off run: a learner that covers its rival's stop is reacting whether or not the reaction pays.

Evidence so farSolved with no noise: blocking off puts every delta at 0, blocking on gives a mixed equilibrium at −0.77 that latest-only can't find and the plan's pool only approaches (0.38 against 0.006). Next: back to the plan, and the verdict.
