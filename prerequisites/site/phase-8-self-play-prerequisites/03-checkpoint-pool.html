The Checkpoint Pool as Approximate Fictitious Play

You can't average the weights of ten networks and get the average of their policies. The plan keeps the networks and samples among them instead.

Your call · a pool of one

The plan's sample() computes cut = max(1, int(0.8 * n)), then with probability 0.7 picks from checkpoints[cut:], otherwise from checkpoints[:cut]. Before episode 1,000 the pool holds one checkpoint.

With n = 1, what does checkpoints[cut:] hold?

Why a pool

Page 02's argument tracks the average of the opponent's strategy. In a matrix game that is a probability over a few actions. For a DQN there is no meaningful way to average ten networks' weights and get a network that plays like their average. The fix: keep a pool of past snapshots and sample an opponent from it each race. The pool's contents are an empirical distribution over past strategies, which stands in for fictitious play's running average without averaging any weights.

The plan's rule

Not a uniform draw. With probability recency_bias = 0.7, sample from the newest 20% of the pool; otherwise from the older 80%. At the full size of 50:

newest slice = 50 − int(0.8 × 50) = 10 checkpoints each 0.7 / 10 = 7% older slice = 40 checkpoints each 0.3 / 40 = 0.75% per checkpoint, newest : older = 0.07 / 0.0075 ≈ 9.3 : 1

That is a trade-off, not a derived number. Too much weight on old snapshots spends training on opponents long outgrown. Too little, and the learner chases its newest self and forgets how to beat older styles: page 01's cycle. max_pool_size = 50 forgets too: the oldest snapshot is evicted on overflow.

The numbers sayNothing

With n = 1, cut = max(1, int(0.8)) = 1, and checkpoints[1:] is empty. Slicing is fine; the failure comes when the 0.7 branch asks np.random.choice to pick from an empty list, which raises ValueError: 691 of 1,000 calls in a measurement. The pool holds one checkpoint for episodes 0 to 999, so the run stops within its first few episodes. The widget on the case brief runs it.

For n = 2 to 5 the newest slice is one checkpoint, and two for n = 6 and 7. The fix is one line: a pool with no newest slice samples from what it has. Lab p8-03's first test is this case.

What the rule costs, measured on the duel

On a game small enough to solve, the cost of each choice can be measured. A pool of exact best replies sampled uniformly and never trimmed is fictitious play, and reaches an exploitability of 0.006 after 1,000 rounds. With the plan's rule it averages 0.38 over the last 200 rounds and never settles; latest-only cycles at 0.75 to 2.26. The window forgets the old strategies the equilibrium mixes in, and the recency weight favours the newest ones, which are still chasing. Below, the leader plays the mixture the plan's rule leaves after 1,000 rounds. Find the lap that beats it: lap 4 earns −0.513, 0.256 more than anything earns against the equilibrium. Networks that generalise change the trade-off, but it isn't free.

The self-play evaluation trap

A self-play reward curve going up proves the learner beats its own recent history. It says nothing about a strategy it has never met, such as Phase 3's rule-based agents or a human. That is why the plan's Definition of Done tests the learner against something outside the loop: "learner's mean finishing position < random agent's".
