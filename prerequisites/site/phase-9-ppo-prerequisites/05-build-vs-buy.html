Why Buy PPO: Loud Failures and Quiet Ones

Knowing when to reach for a library is a skill worth being able to explain. So is knowing what a library can't do for you.

Your call · five PPOs

Lab p9-05 trains the same small PPO five ways on the toy race: correct, with the advantage's sign flipped, with the old probabilities re-read every pass, with GAE bootstrapping from the wrong state, and without standardising the advantages.

Judged by its training curve alone, which bug gives itself away?

Phases 4 and 5 built; Phase 9 buys

Q-learning's and DQN's update rules are a few lines each and easy to test in isolation: does one update move one value the right way by about the right amount? Getting them wrong tends to fail loudly: divergence, nonsense values, a curve flat at a clearly wrong level.

PPO's details fail quietly:

  • GAE's backward pass (page 03): an off-by-one, or the wrong value at an episode end, biases every advantage in the batch. Nothing crashes; the curve is a little worse, for ever.
  • Advantage standardisation: in every working PPO, SB3's included, and in none of page 04's equations. Leaving it out changes how hard the clip bites, because the clip's effect depends on the scale of A.
  • Old probabilities: πθ_old must be cached when the batch is collected. Re-reading it from the current policy makes every ratio 1, so the clip never binds.
  • Initialisation: orthogonal weights for both networks matter for stability and appear in no objective.

The principle: build when failures are loud, because loud failures are cheap to catch by testing and looking. Buy when they are quiet, because a widely used library has had those bugs shaken out by thousands of users who hit them first. That argument is the plan's README section, "knowing when to build from scratch versus when to reach for a battle-tested library".

The flipped sign drives the policy away from good calls and the curve shows it at once. The other three improve from about 245–260 s to about 234–237 s, which looks like learning, and still end 9 to 12 s from the optimum, where the correct one ends 1.9 s away. Only an exact ruler or a targeted test reveals them; the lab has you write the three tests.

The raw-reward run of the case is the quiet kind: no error, no warning, a curve that is flat at a level you can only call wrong if you know the optimum.

What buying doesn't buy

A library can be trusted to compute GAE, standardise, cache and clip. It can't know what a sensible scale for your reward is, or that your critic is starting 263 seconds from the truth. Those are inputs, and the plan chooses them. A battle-tested algorithm fed an input it handles badly fails as quietly as a buggy one.

Evidence so farJ3 is a quiet failure, and of a kind the library can't catch: the algorithm is right, the input is the plan's. Next: inside .learn(), and the critic's pace.

Loud and quiet

Page 05's argument, measured. The same small PPO, trained five ways on the toy race: one correct, one with a loud bug, three with the quiet bugs the page lists. First you judge them the way a training log lets you, by their curves alone, and then see how far each really ended from the optimum. Then you write the three small tests that catch the quiet ones, each graded against its own bug.

Environment: an F1-flavoured mini: the 20-lap toy race, learned by the table-policy PPO in labs/labkit/minipg.py and scored exactly against the race's optimum. The given script runs in under a minute.
