What is the agent told each lap?

Reinforcement learning has one loop, and everything else is built on it:

  1. The environment hands the agent an observation: the situation as the agent sees it.
  2. The agent picks an action.
  3. The environment applies it, moves on, and hands back a reward (one number for how good that step was) and the next observation.
  4. Repeat until the episode ends.

On the Pit Wall, the observation is the panel: lap, tyre, tyre age, the flags. The action is one of the three buttons. The reward is minus the lap time, so a 10.5 s lap pays −10.5. The agent's job is to learn a policy, a rule from observation to action, that makes the total reward as large as possible.

Nobody tells the agent that A falls off a cliff after 6 laps or that a stop costs 4 s. It has to work the rules out from rewards. The real project is the same, only bigger: 16 numbers in, one of six calls out, -lap_time back.

One lap's reward can point the wrong way

Take the same safety car on lap 6, but this time you are still on the A tyres you started on: six laps old, at the cliff. Staying out pays −14.0 for the lap, since every lap behind the safety car takes 14 s. Boxing for B pays −15.6: the same 14 s plus the 1.6 s stop. On this lap's reward alone, staying out wins.

Over the rest of the race it loses. Counting laps 6 to 20, staying out costs 180.92 s on average and boxing 180.52 s. The stop pays for itself, but only later, in laps the lap-6 reward knows nothing about. Race #49 below has that safety car on lap 6. Play it once staying out on lap 6 and once boxing (Same dice again replays it), and compare the two totals at the flag.

Same event, opposite calls. The right call depends on the whole situation, not on the safety car alone. And neither answer shows in the lap-6 reward, where staying out always looks 1.6 s better. That gap between one reward and the whole race is what the rest of Phase 1 is about.

Can every goal be one number per lap?

RL assumes so. The reward hypothesis says any goal can be written as maximising the expected sum of one number per step. If you cannot squeeze what you want into that number, you do not have an RL problem yet. You have a reward-design problem first.

The goal here is the shortest race, and the reward is -lap_time on every lap. Added up over a race, that is minus the race time, because the race time is the sum of the lap times, pit stops included. So the agent collects the most reward when, and only when, it finishes fastest. Phase 1's plan spends a section making sure there is no way to collect reward that is not real race time.

Deep diveIs a cheap safety-car stop a loophole?70 words

A loophole would be reward the agent can collect without going faster. The safety-car discount is not one: a stop behind the safety car loses less time in the real race too, so learning to pit there is learning real strategy. The sum of -lap_time equals -total_race_time however the agent gets there. That is the property the plan checks, and the same check comes back in Phase 5's reward-shaping decisions.

Where does a race end?

An episode is one run of the loop, start to finish. Some tasks never end (a thermostat); this one does. One episode is one race, lap 1 to the flag, a fixed length. Gymnasium has two end signals: terminated (the task finished) and truncated (cut off for an outside reason, such as a time limit). Phase 1 sets truncated = False always: the episode ends when the race does.

Try something new, or use what works?

At every call the agent can exploit, taking the action it thinks is best, or explore, trying another to find out. Too much of the first and it stays stuck on its first decent strategy; too much of the second and it never uses what it learned. Phase 1 trains no agent, but it shapes how hard this is.

Deep diveThe curriculum hook: start with an easy race60 words

reset(options={"curriculum": "dry_no_sc"}) restricts a race to dry weather with no safety car. An agent can learn the basic pit window on that smaller, less noisy problem first and meet the hard version once the easy one is solved. This is curriculum learning. Exploration itself is the agent's job, from Phase 3 on; Phase 1 only provides the hook.

Why is the safety car rolled fresh every race?

Press Same dice again on race #49 a few times. Once you know the safety car comes on lap 6, you can wait for it and look brilliant. You have memorised one race, not learned strategy. An agent trained on a handful of fixed races does the same: it sees laps_remaining, so it can learn "the safety car comes around lap 12 in race 2". A lookup table dressed as a policy.

That failure is called environment overfitting, a form of reward hacking: scoring well by exploiting how the environment was built, not by solving the problem. So the plan splits the two sources of chance. Weather is replayed from real races, where variety is the point. Safety cars are drawn fresh every episode from a hazard rate, where memorising them would be a bug. The Pit Wall's New race button does the same.
