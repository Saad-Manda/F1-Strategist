On-Policy vs. Off-Policy: SARSA

Change exactly one term in Q-learning's update and the algorithm starts to account for its own future clumsiness. That single term is the entire difference between off-policy and on-policy learning.

SARSA's update

Q(s, a) ← Q(s, a) + α [ r + γ · Q(s', a') − Q(s, a) ]

Set next to Q-learning's update from page 2, the only change is in the target:

Target
Q-learningr + γ · max_a' Q(s', a')
SARSAr + γ · Q(s', a') — a' is the action actually taken next

Q-learning's target asks "what's the best I could possibly do from s'?" SARSA's target asks "what will I actually do from s', given that I'm still an ε-greedy agent who sometimes acts randomly?" a' in SARSA isn't a hypothetical best action — it's whatever the agent's own ε-greedy policy actually selects as its next move, which the agent commits to before computing this step's update (the "pre-commit" mechanic page 7 builds and page 9 traces through the real code).

On-policy, defined precisely

SARSA's name is literally its signature: State, Action, Reward, State, Action — the five-tuple (s, a, r, s', a') its update consumes, with the final a' coming from the same policy generating the data. That's what on-policy means: the target is computed using the same policy (ε-greedy) that's actually producing the agent's behavior, not a hypothetical greedy policy it isn't really following yet. Q-learning is off-policy for the opposite reason — its target always assumes the greedy policy, regardless of what the ε-greedy behavior policy actually does next.

The practical consequence: SARSA's learned values account for the real chance that the agent's own future exploration could stumble into something costly. Q-learning's don't — its target is blind to what its behavior policy actually samples, so it never "pays for" the risk of its own exploration inside the value it's learning.

Worked example: a risky push vs. a safe pit, hand-run side by side

The standard textbook cliff-walking contrast, in this project's own flavor. A decision point s1 with two choices: pit now (known, safe cost) or push one more lap (cheaper if followed by a clean pit, but one lap closer to the tire cliff):

s1, PIT_NOW → r = −12, terminal (safe: known cost, race over) s1, PUSH → r = −1, next = s2 (cheap this lap, but risk waits at s2) s2, PIT → r = −8, terminal (recover cleanly: the good outcome) s2, PUSH_MORE→ r = −30, terminal (the cliff: tire failure)

Ignoring exploration entirely, the greedy-optimal policy is clear: PUSH then PIT costs −1 + (−8) = −9, beating PIT_NOW's −12 outright. But an ε-greedy agent training at s2 doesn't always pick PIT — sometimes, by exploration, it picks PUSH_MORE and pays −30. Whether that real risk gets priced into Q(s1, PUSH) is exactly what separates the two algorithms. Hand-run both from identical rewards, α = 0.5, γ = 1, tables starting at 0, across three training episodes:

1Explores into PUSH_MORE (r=−30). Both tables start at 0, so Q-learning's max and SARSA's actual a' agree by coincidence (both point at an untrained 0-cell).−0.5−0.5
2Now Q(s2,PIT)=0 > Q(s2,PUSH_MORE)=−15, so both the greedy max and the ε-greedy draw land on PIT (r=−8) — still no divergence.−0.75−0.75
3An unlucky exploratory draw hits PUSH_MORE again (r=−30). Q-learning's target still uses max(Q(s2,PIT)=−4, Q(s2,PUSH_MORE)=−15) = −4 — it ignores that PUSH_MORE is what actually happened. SARSA's target plugs in Q(s2,PUSH_MORE)=−15 directly, because that's the action it actually took next.−2.875−8.375

By the end of episode 3 the two tables have visibly diverged: Q-learning's estimate for PUSH barely registers the close call with the cliff, because its target is computed as if the agent will always play the best available action afterward — even on the very episode where it didn't. SARSA's estimate is punished directly, because its target is built from what the agent actually did next, exploration and all. Run this same contrast for thousands of episodes (page 7's exercise) and the pattern holds: Q-learning converges toward the aggressive, optimal-if-perfectly-executed policy (PUSH, trusting that PIT will always be chosen afterward); SARSA converges toward a more cautious policy that accounts for the real, nonzero chance its own future self stumbles into the cliff while still exploring.

Tying back to the real plan

This is why the real Definition of Done trains and compares both agents on the same races rather than picking whichever seems obviously better in the abstract — the difference between their learned policies is itself the lesson, not a redundant extra experiment. Q-learning is expected to learn the theoretically optimal policy faster, since its target has no interest in how risky its own exploration is; SARSA is expected to learn something a bit more conservative, since its values are shaped by the exploration it's actually doing at training time. Neither is "more correct" — they're answers to two different questions (page 9's Definition of Done note frames it exactly this way), and the comparison notebook exists to make the gap between them visible on real race data, not just on a three-state toy.
